Current Ph.D. research

Cotton flower detection in UAV field imagery

Mississippi State University · 2026 to present · USDA drone imagery

The problem

Flowering is one of the most useful signals a cotton breeder or agronomist can track: it tells you how a plot is developing and how it responds to its conditions. Today the count is usually made by people walking the rows, which is slow, hard to repeat, and impossible to scale across large trials. Drones can photograph a whole field in minutes, but the images still have to be turned into numbers.

Why it is hard

Flower pixels are a tiny minority of every frame. Illumination changes across a plot and through the day, leaves catch the sun and look bright, and soil and shadow shift the colours around them. A detector that simply looks for "white" will count glare and specks as flowers, and one that is too strict will miss flowers that are partly hidden by the canopy.

My approach

I build the pipeline in Python with scikit-learn, scikit-image and OpenCV, and every stage exists to fix a specific failure of the one before it:

  1. Pixel classificationA logistic-regression model scores each pixel as flower or background (canopy, soil, shadow).
  2. Morphological cleanupOpening removes isolated specks; closing rejoins flowers that were fragmented.
  3. Connected componentsLabelling turns the cleaned mask into individual candidate flowers.
  4. PCA shape filteringPrincipal-component shape descriptors reject streaks and glare that are too long or too small to be flowers.

Why interpretable

I chose a linear classifier and explicit geometric filters on purpose. When the count is wrong, you can see exactly why: which pixels scored high, which blobs survived cleanup, and which shape test let a false positive through. That makes the thresholds tunable as new field imagery arrives, and it makes the result something an agronomist can trust rather than a black box.

Co-authored publication · 2026

AI and data science for financial decision-making

Asian Journal of Advanced Research and Reports, 20(9), 262-280

The question

Firms invest in AI expecting better decisions, but it is rarely clear how that investment turns into performance. This study asks whether AI adoption improves business performance directly, or through the quality of financial decisions it enables, and at what point the effect appears.

Data and models

We built a decision-support framework on a synthetic panel of 1,200 enterprise profiles across six industrial sectors, covering 2019 to 2025, and compared eight approaches: multiple linear regression, support vector regression, random forest, gradient boosting, XGBoost, artificial neural networks and structural equation modelling, with SHAP for explanation.

0.914R-squared, XGBoost, Business Performance Index
3.623RMSE
2.541MAE
8Modelling approaches benchmarked

What we found

XGBoost was the strongest predictor, explaining 91.4% of the variance in the Business Performance Index. Structural equation modelling showed that the Financial Decision Score mediates the link between AI adoption and performance, and SHAP revealed a threshold effect: the performance benefit strengthens once AI adoption passes a certain level.

How we checked it

The framework was validated through train-test partitioning, hyperparameter optimisation, cross-validation, noise-injection robustness testing, sector-specific validation and sensitivity analysis.

How it connects to my research

The common thread with my imaging work is explainability. In both, a strong model is only half the job; the other half is showing why it decided what it decided, so that someone can act on the result with confidence.

Open source · Math education

Errata

Python · computer algebra

Finds the first wrong step in a student's own mathematical derivation, without solving the problem for them and without silently fixing their mistakes. Correctness is decided by computer algebra rather than a language model, so every verdict is reproducible and explainable.

  • Treats each equation as the zero set of its residual, so one quotient test tells whether a step kept, lost or added solutions.
  • Separates error types (arithmetic, sign, lost roots, extraneous roots, domain restrictions) and explains each in plain language.
  • Abstains with an explicit "unknown" when the algebra cannot decide, instead of guessing.
